# Literature Review

**Topic:** Can social media usage patterns predict mental health outcomes? (Overconsumption of low-quality digital content)

---

## 1. Demystifying the new dilemma of brain rot in the digital era: A review

**Authors:** Yousef, Alshamy, Tlili, Metwally (2025), *Brain Sciences*
**Link:** [add link here]

This review looks at "brain rot," meaning mental exhaustion and weaker thinking linked to heavy exposure to low-quality online material. It connects this kind of overconsumption to distress, anxiety, depression, and problems with memory and decision-making. The authors also suggest remedies such as limiting screen time and choosing content more carefully.

**Relevance to my project:** It defines the "low-grade content" idea my topic is built on and supports using usage-related variables as predictors.

---

## 2. Impact of Digital Devices on Mental Health Leading to Brain Rot in Higher Education Students

**Authors:** Roy & Banerjee (2025), SSRN preprint
**Link:** [add link here]

The paper studies college students and describes how aimless scrolling, short attention spans, and a craving for quick rewards go along with mental tiredness, anxiety, and emotional instability. It names low-quality social media content as a frequent trigger of these problems. Because it is a preprint, it has not been through peer review.

**Relevance to my project:** Its sample (students) is similar to many public datasets, so it helps justify my population and variables. I will treat its findings with some caution because of its preprint status.

---

## 3. Impacts of digital social media detox for mental health: A systematic review and meta-analysis

**Authors:** Ramadhan, Rampengan, Yumnanisha et al. (2024), *Narra J*
**Link:** [add link here]

This meta-analysis pools studies in which people deliberately cut back on social media. It found a clear drop in depressive symptoms, but no statistically significant change in life satisfaction, stress, or overall well-being. So the benefits of reducing use appear real for some outcomes and not others.

**Relevance to my project:** It shows that the link between usage and mental health depends on which outcome is measured, so I should be careful about my choice of target variable and not expect every outcome to be equally predictable.

---

## 4. From screens to serenity: evaluating the effect of digital detox on mental and physiological health

**Authors:** Farrukh, Reza, Babar, Alam, Imtiaz (2025), *BMC Medical Education*
**Link:** [add link here]

The researchers ran a randomized controlled trial with medical students who had high screen time. A two-week digital detox, combined with other activities, improved stress and anxiety scores and also shifted physical markers such as heart rate variability and cortisol. This is stronger evidence than a survey because participants were randomly assigned.

**Relevance to my project:** It gives causal evidence that my correlational dataset cannot, and it shows that the usage-to-stress relationship has a real physiological basis.

---

## 5. Mental health concerns in the digital age

**Authors:** Scott, Valley, Simecka (2017), *International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction*
**Link:** [add link here]

This paper argues that overusing technology can affect people's behavior, emotions, and thinking. It points to information overload and constant connectivity as contributors to stress, anxiety, and depression, and it reviews counseling approaches for restoring balance. It is an older, broad overview rather than a new experiment.

**Relevance to my project:** It gives the general background for why technology overuse is treated as a mental health issue, and it comes from before the recent "brain rot" wave of research.

---

## Summary and Gap

Across these sources, heavy or low-quality digital consumption is repeatedly linked to anxiety, stress, and depression, and reducing use often helps, although the effect differs by outcome (source 3). Most of the evidence is either review-based or from small student samples, and "content quality" is rarely measured directly. My project therefore uses the closest available proxies in my dataset (passive vs. active use, dominant content type, social-comparison triggers) alongside screen time, late-night use, and sleep. It tests how well they predict a validated anxiety score (GAD-7), and whether content-related variables add predictive value beyond simple screen time.
